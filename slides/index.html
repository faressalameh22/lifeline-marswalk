Never go farther than you can walk home.

LIFELINE keeps every Marswalk within reach of home, even when the rover or a suit fails.

A Marswalk planner on real terrain of the boxwork ridges of Mount Sharp, Gale Crater. It checks every minute of the walk and gives one answer: GO or NO-GO, and why.

The problem

On Mars no rescue team can come, so a crew far from the habitat must always be able to get home on its own.

The user: the person who plans a crewed walk (EVA) on Mars.

The decision: GO or NO-GO for a walk to three science stops in the boxwork of Mount Sharp, and what to do on the way when something goes wrong.

What is missing: a map shows where to go. It does not show whether the crew can still get back, minute by minute, if the rover breaks down or a suit fails.

Stops A and B are where Curiosity drilled. Stop C has never been reached: wheels struggle on its steep edges, boots do not.

The idea

We apply Apollo's rule, "never go farther than you can walk home", to Mars through three limits that must hold at every minute.

If the rover fails

1. Walk-back

The suit reserve left must cover the walk home over the real terrain, plus a 20% safety pad.

Rule, activity rates and 20% pad: Apollo traverse planning.

If a suit fails

2. Ride-back

The emergency oxygen (0.95 h, 57 min) must cover the ride home at 10 km/h, plus the 20% pad.

0.95 h and 10 km/h: Apollo traverse planning.

Always

3. Time in suit

The whole walk must end inside the time an astronaut can stay in the suit: 4.25 h.

4.25 h is a team assumption, not yet sourced.

Farthest stop first

The crew drives to the farthest stop and works back, so the walk home gets shorter as the suits run down. Gates at each stop switch to plan B (skip the lowest-science stop) or plan C (go home).

Data and validation

Real Mars terrain and Curiosity's own track make the margins trustworthy, and we show openly where they are not.

Layered data, two agencies, two spacecraft

  • Terrain: 30 m HRSC elevation mosaic of Gale Crater, ESA Mars Express (Persaud et al. 2021, CC BY 4.0)
  • Rover track and stops: NASA's Curiosity waypoint file, 885 points in our map
  • Images: Curiosity Mastcam and Hazcam (A, B), MRO HiRISE from orbit (C)
  • Weather: Curiosity REMS, 123 sols between stops A and B: air −82 to −5 °C, 679 to 782 Pa
  • Radiation: Curiosity RAD, 0.67 mSv/day
  • Limits and rates: Apollo traverse planning

Impact and next steps

LIFELINE turns a crew's safety margin into a number they can check before and during every Marswalk.

Before the walk

GO or NO-GO with the reason, fixes that never cut the safety pad, and a printed backup card with leave-by times in case the tool itself fails.

During the walk

A wrist display (right): margin, walk home, next stop, turn-back time, active plan. Gates at each stop decide when to skip a stop or go home.

Next steps

  • Find sources for walking speed and suit capacity, the two assumptions that matter most
  • A sand layer from THEMIS thermal data, where walking is slower
  • A second rover or support crew, to cover double failures
  • A helmet display for the walking crew
  • The same method works at any landing site with an elevation model
